One of the most persistent myths about healing is that it moves in a straight line. The five stages of grief — denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance — were introduced by Elisabeth Kübler-Ross in 1969 to describe what she observed in terminally ill patients. Her intention was never to prescribe a sequence. She described them as non-linear, frequently overlapping, and not universally experienced. Yet the popular understanding hardened them into a checklist: complete stage one, move to stage two, arrive at acceptance and be done.

Real healing does not work that way. The more accurate shape is a spiral. You return to the same emotional territory — grief, anger, confusion, sadness — but each time you return, you are not in exactly the same place. You are slightly further along. The pain may feel identical, but your capacity to be with it has changed. What felt like drowning on the first pass may, six months later, feel like wading. That is not regression. That is the spiral doing its work.

This matters because setbacks are one of the most common reasons people conclude they are not healing. They feel fine for weeks, and then a song, a date on the calendar, or a chance encounter undoes them completely. The feeling is: I'm back at square one. But square one no longer exists. The person experiencing that wave of grief is not the same person who entered this process. They are bringing a different set of resources to the same emotional material.

There is also an important distinction between processing pain and ruminating on it. Processing involves turning toward the emotion, feeling it, and allowing it to move. Rumination involves cycling through the same thoughts without forward movement — replaying events, rehearsing arguments, narrating injury. Processing hurts and helps. Rumination hurts and stalls. Learning to tell the difference is part of the work.

If your pain is severe, persistent, or interfering significantly with daily functioning, professional support from a therapist or counselor is not a last resort — it is appropriate and wise.

## Reflection Prompt

Where in the spiral do you feel you are today? Not where you think you should be — where you actually are.
