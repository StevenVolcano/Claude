Emotional pain is not only in the mind. Bessel van der Kolk's foundational work established what many people had long sensed but struggled to articulate: the body keeps the score. Grief, trauma, and loss leave physical traces — patterns of tension, altered posture, changed breathing, disrupted sleep. The nervous system registers what the conscious mind may not yet have processed.

This is not metaphor. When the threat-response system is activated — by conflict, loss, fear, or overwhelm — the body mobilizes for action: muscles contract, breathing shortens, digestion slows, attention narrows. If the event resolves, the body discharges that activation and returns to baseline. But when an experience is too large or too sudden to process in the moment, that activation can become frozen in the body. The event is over, but the physiology of the event continues to run.

Common somatic signs of unprocessed grief or emotional pain include chronic tightness in the chest or throat, a persistent heaviness in the body, fatigue that sleep doesn't resolve, numbness or a sense of being "behind glass," and a hair-trigger startle response. These are not character weaknesses. They are the body reporting on what it is holding.

The connection between body and emotion is bidirectional. Changing your body state — through breath, movement, or physical touch — genuinely changes your emotional state. This is why somatic practices are not supplemental to emotional healing. They are often the fastest path into it.

Somatic awareness begins with the simple act of noticing. Where is there tension right now? What is the quality of your breath — shallow or full, fast or slow? Where in your body do you feel the emotion you are currently carrying? Naming a physical sensation with specificity — "there is a tight band across my chest" — shifts the brain from reactive mode toward observation and regulation.

## Grounding Exercise: 3-Minute Body Scan

Find a comfortable seated position with your feet on the floor. Close your eyes or soften your gaze.

**Minute 1:** Start at the top of your head and slowly move your attention downward — scalp, jaw, neck, shoulders. Notice any tension without trying to change it. Just observe.

**Minute 2:** Continue down — chest, stomach, lower back, hips. When you find an area of tightness or discomfort, breathe toward it. Imagine the exhale softening that place slightly.

**Minute 3:** If emotion arises as you scan, that is expected. Let it be present. Name what you notice: "tightness," "heaviness," "a catch in my chest." You are not required to feel anything in particular — you are only required to notice what is actually there.

When you finish, take one full breath and open your eyes slowly.
