Most people believe they are listening when they are actually waiting. They track the gist of what is being said just well enough to know when to speak — and meanwhile, part of their attention is already drafting a response, preparing a counterpoint, or planning a defense. This is passive listening: the words arrive, but full understanding doesn't.

Active listening is something different. It requires the listener to be genuinely engaged with the goal of understanding — not just the content of what's being said, but the emotion and need behind it. That requires attention, which is harder than it sounds when you are also managing your own reactions.

Active listening has three components: attend, understand, and respond. Attending means giving your full physical presence — facing the speaker, setting down distractions, making eye contact at a natural level. Understanding means tracking not just words but tone, pacing, and what might be underneath the surface. Responding means feeding back what you heard — not your interpretation, not your reaction, but the substance and feeling of what was communicated.

Paraphrasing is one of the most powerful tools in active listening, and one of the most misunderstood. It is not a trick or a therapeutic technique. It is simply reflecting back what you heard — "So what you're saying is..." — to give the speaker the chance to confirm, correct, or go deeper. When someone feels accurately heard, their nervous system relaxes. Defense drops. Real conversation becomes possible.

Signaling that you're listening doesn't require words. A small nod, a brief "mm-hmm," leaning slightly forward — these nonverbal cues tell the speaker that you are with them, without interrupting their train of thought.

The WAIT acronym is a useful internal check: **Why Am I Talking?** Before speaking in a sensitive conversation, pause and ask whether your contribution moves understanding forward or simply redirects attention to yourself.

## Exercise: The 2-Minute Listening Drill

Sit with a partner and invite them to speak for two uninterrupted minutes on anything — how their day went, something on their mind. Your only job is to listen. When they finish, paraphrase back what you heard: what was the main point, and what feeling seemed to be underneath it. Do not add your own view, opinion, or comparable experience. If you are practicing alone, record a voice note of yourself describing something on your mind, then play it back and write a paraphrase of what you heard.
