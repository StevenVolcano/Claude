Research from UCLA led by Matthew Lieberman found that labeling an emotion — simply naming what you feel with specificity — reduces its intensity. Brain imaging showed that affect labeling decreases activity in the amygdala (the alarm center) and increases activity in the prefrontal cortex (the seat of reasoning and regulation). Putting a word to what you feel is not just descriptive. It is regulatory. The act of naming shifts the brain from reactive to observational.

Most people have a limited emotional vocabulary. They know "mad," "sad," "stressed," and "fine." This is a problem because what gets called "stressed" might be a mix of anxiety, resentment, shame, and exhaustion — each with different causes and different appropriate responses. Collapsing all of those into a single word makes it impossible to respond specifically. This is what researchers call emotional granularity: the capacity to distinguish between closely related emotional states.

A person with high emotional granularity who feels "bad" can identify: "I'm disappointed that the plan fell through, and I'm also anxious about what comes next, and underneath that there's some shame because I think I could have handled it better." Each of those emotions points somewhere different. Disappointment calls for grief and adjustment. Anxiety calls for information or reassurance. Shame calls for self-compassion and an honest look at what actually happened. High granularity enables targeted response. Low granularity produces a generalized reactive state.

Primary emotions are the initial, unmediated feelings that arise directly from an event — fear, hurt, sadness, joy. Secondary emotions are reactions to having the primary emotion — shame about feeling angry, anxiety about feeling sad, numbness as a defense against overwhelm. Many people live primarily in secondary emotions without accessing what is underneath.

The connection between unacknowledged mood and reactive behavior in relationships is direct. When we do not know what we are feeling, we act it out instead. Irritability, withdrawal, sarcasm, and flatness are frequently the behavioral expression of emotions that have not been named.

## Exercise: The 60-Second Mood Check-In

Before any important or potentially tense conversation today, pause for 60 seconds. Ask yourself three questions: What am I feeling right now, with as much specificity as possible? Where in my body is that feeling located? Is there anything I'm carrying into this conversation from earlier today that isn't about this conversation?

You do not need to resolve anything in 60 seconds. You just need to know where you are before you walk in.
