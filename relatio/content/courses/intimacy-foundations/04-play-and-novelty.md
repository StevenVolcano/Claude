Arthur Aron and his colleagues at the Stony Brook University Relationship Lab conducted a series of experiments in which couples were assigned either routine activities or "self-expanding" novel activities to do together. The results were consistent: couples who engaged in novel, challenging activities together reported significantly higher relationship quality and felt more in love than those who repeated familiar routines. Novelty reactivates attraction.

The reason has a neurological basis. The early stages of a relationship are rich in novel experience — every conversation reveals something new, every shared activity is the first of its kind. The brain is flooded with dopamine, the chemical of seeking and anticipation. As a relationship matures and the terrain becomes familiar, that dopamine response diminishes. This is natural and not a problem — the nervous system cannot sustain early-stage intensity indefinitely. But it does mean that some degree of intentional novelty is required to keep the neurochemistry of attraction engaged.

Play has similar properties. Genuine play — activities done for their own sake, with full engagement and no fixed outcome — reactivates neurological states that feel like early-relationship energy. Couples who play together tend to face each other with more generosity and lightness. Laughter is a potent social bonding mechanism. Shared silliness communicates trust.

Why do couples stop playing? Busyness is the most common explanation, but it rarely tells the full story. Self-consciousness plays a role — the fear of looking foolish in front of someone whose opinion matters. Gender norms can restrict both partners in different directions. The social pressure to be productive can make play feel like a luxury rather than a necessity.

There is also a distinction worth making between genuine play and humor used as avoidance — using jokes to deflect vulnerability rather than to connect. The difference lies in whether the play brings you closer or keeps things safely surface-level.

## Exercise: One Novel Activity This Week

Identify one activity that neither of you has done before — something slightly outside your usual range, not necessarily dramatic. Take a class, try a route you've never walked, cook a cuisine neither of you knows, go somewhere new. Plan it for this week. The activity itself matters less than the decision to do something unfamiliar together.
